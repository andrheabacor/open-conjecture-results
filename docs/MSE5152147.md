# Square sums of modular remainders over divisors

**SOLVED — Math Stack Exchange 5152147.** For every integer \(x>13\), there is a positive \(y<x\) for which the sum of \(x\bmod d\) over the positive divisors \(d\) of \(y\) is either **4 or 9**. On the original domain \(x\ge2\), the exact exceptions to the existence of a square greater than 1 are \(2,3,4,5,6,13\). Lean 4 checked both results. [Original question](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/5152147/existence-of-a-non-trivial-perfect-square-sum-of-modular-remainders-over-divisor) · [Lean proof](../OpenConjectures/MSE5152147.lean)

## Source and prior contribution

The author, **pie**, posted the conjecture on 18 October 2026. With \(S(x,y)=\sum_{d\mid y,\;d>0}(x\bmod d)\), the displayed conjecture reads as follows. [Original revision](https://math.stackexchange.com/revisions/5152147/1)

> \(\forall x \in \mathbb{N}_{> 13}, \exists y \in \mathbb{N} \setminus \{0\} \text{ such that } y < x \text{ and } S(x, y) = k^2 \text{ for some } k \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 2}\)

The two public comments, both by **lulu** on 18 October, establish that \(S(x,y)\) depends only on \(x\bmod y\), and that a prime divisor \(p>3\) of \(x-4\) gives \(S(x,p)=4\). The proof below uses both observations. The additional step is to handle every remaining factorization of \(x-4\) using two prime cubes and three explicit cases. [Residue-class comment](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/5152147/#comment11127625_5152147) · [Prime-divisor comment](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/5152147/#comment11127632_5152147)

## Proof

Fix \(x>13\) and put \(m=x-4\), so \(m>9\). Whenever \(y\mid m\), every divisor \(d\) of \(y\) divides \(x-4\). Hence \(x\bmod d=4\bmod d\) for each summand and \(S(x,y)=S(4,y)\). Each positive divisor \(y\) of \(m\) satisfies \(y\le m<x\).

1. If \(m\) has a prime divisor \(p\ge5\), take \(y=p\). Its divisors are \(1,p\), giving \(S(x,p)=0+4=4\).
2. If \(8\mid m\), take \(y=8\). The divisors \(1,2,4,8\) give \(S(4,8)=0+0+0+4=4\). If \(27\mid m\), take \(y=27\). The divisors \(1,3,9,27\) give \(S(4,27)=0+1+4+4=9\).
3. Otherwise \(m\) has only the prime factors 2 and 3, each with exponent at most 2. Thus \(m\mid 2^2 3^2=36\). The divisors of 36 above 9 are **12, 18, 36**, giving \(x=16,22,40\). For \(x=16,40\), take \(y=12\). Both inputs are congruent to 4 modulo 12, and the sum over \(1,2,3,4,6,12\) is \(0+0+1+0+4+4=9\). For \(x=22\), take \(y=13\), giving \(0+(22\bmod13)=9\).

These cases exhaust every \(x>13\). For the exceptional-set assertion, inputs \(7,8,9,10,11,12\) give sum 4 with respective witnesses \(4,6,5,8,4,8\). For each of \(2,3,4,5,6,13\), every admissible \(y\) gives a sum below 9 and different from 4. In particular, the totals for \(x=13\), in order \(y=1,\ldots,12\), are \(0,1,1,2,3,3,6,7,5,7,2,5\).

## Source correspondence and verification

`remainderSum x y` is a sum over Mathlib's `Nat.divisors y`. The conjecture's `1 ≤ y` ensures this is exactly the set of positive divisors of \(y\). `mse_5152147` has the source's unrestricted natural-number quantifiers, strict bounds on \(x,y\), and \(k\ge2\). `four_or_nine` proves the stronger choice of square and `exact_exceptions` proves the classification for every \(x\ge2\). The finite checks in the proof use kernel reduction with `decide`. [Lean source](../OpenConjectures/MSE5152147.lean)

**One printed example has an arithmetic error.** For \(x=7,y=6\), the source lists the correct summands, but \(0+1+1+1=3\). The witness \(y=4\) gives the intended square. Its second example, \(S(11,4)=4\), is correct. Three Lean sanity lemmas check these values with `by decide`. ## Independent review

A reviewer recompiled the file and found no `sorry`, `admit`, `native_decide` or added axioms. They checked that `remainderSum x y = ∑ d ∈ y.divisors, x % d` matches the question's S(x, y), and that `mse_5152147` uses the question's quantifiers (x > 13, 1 ≤ y < x, a square k² with k ≥ 2). They ran a separate brute-force search, which gives exactly the exceptions {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 13} for 2 ≤ x ≤ 3000, and confirmed that the question's first example is miscomputed (S(7, 6) = 3, while S(7, 4) = 4). On 27 Nov 2026 the question had no answers. It had two comments by lulu, which suggest reducing x modulo y and observe that any x with a prime p > 3 dividing x − 4 works. The proof completes exactly that suggestion, so the key idea should be credited to lulu. Searches of the solved-conjectures and TruReTuring catalogues, aiXiv and Math.SE found no earlier full proof.
